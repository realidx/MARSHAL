\label{sec:diagnose}

We ask whether Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 \citep{yang2025qwen3} can infer a partner's preference
from interaction and use that inference to choose a valuable action.
A poor final decision could arise because the model misreads its partner,
because it chooses poorly even given a correct reading, or because both steps
fail. We therefore examine the two capabilities at the same decision, and
replace the model's explicit inference answer to test whether its choice
depends on that answer.

\paragraph{Inferring from partner behavior.}
We evaluate 16 held-out two-player structures, eight with binary goals and
eight with linear goals. Each case contains a partner offer chosen under the
fixed reference policy, followed by the focal player's response. The partner's
offer is evidence about its private preference: the oracle weights each
candidate preference by the probability of that offer under the reference
policy and updates the prior accordingly. The focal player's own response
changes the current commitments. The oracle reports a qualitative judgment
$J_c^\star$ which means the preferences still possible and, when one has a sufficiently
clear posterior lead, the favored preference. It also computes the expected
terminal value $V_c(a)=Q_i^\star(I_c,a)$ of every legal action under the fixed
reference continuation. For every retained case, a certificate verifies that
all posteriors compatible with $J_c^\star$ have the same optimal-action set.
Thus omitted posterior magnitudes cannot explain a planning error when the
correct judgment is supplied. The label rule and certificate are described
in Appendix~\ref{app:diagnose}.

\paragraph{Intervening on the judgment.}
For each case $c$, we first ask the model to infer a judgment $\widehat J_c$
from the visible history. We then present the current decision in two fresh
planning calls: one supplies $\widehat J_c$, and the other supplies the
oracle judgment $J_c^\star$. Both calls omit the behavioral history and have
everything else unchanged. A separate call asks the model to choose directly
from the history. We measure decision quality by oracle regret and the paired
change in value:
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{Reg}_c(a)=\max_{a'}V_c(a')-V_c(a),\qquad
 \Delta_c=V_c\!\left(\widehat a(J_c^\star)\right)
          -V_c\!\left(\widehat a(\widehat J_c)\right).
 \label{eq:diagnose-regret}
\end{equation}
Here a positive $\Delta_c$ means that supplying the correct judgment
improves the model's chosen action. Both actions in the comparison are
scored under the same oracle posterior.

\paragraph{Two failures and their interface.}
Table~\ref{tab:diagnose-main} reports the results on the 16 histories. The
model never produces the exact qualitative inference judgment. Even when
given the oracle judgment, it chooses an optimal action in only $1/16$ cases
(mean regret $0.844$). Direct choice from the full history is optimal in
$1/15$ cases with a valid action. These observations identify failures in
both evaluated interfaces under the fixed reference policy. Replacing the judgment changes the model's action in $11/12$ valid paired
comparisons, so the explicit inference input affects its choice. The
replacement improves value in only one case and has a mean paired change of
$-0.042$. Thus a correct judgment does not reliably repair this model's
planning on the selected histories. The intervention concerns a judgment
passed between calls; it makes no claim about separable internal modules.

\paragraph{An illustrative interaction.}
Consider a linear-goal case (\texttt{linear-12231}) in which Blair's
preference for \textsc{Orchard} is hidden. Orchard scores the average
completion of Alex's Cedar and Blair's Cedar commitments. Blair also wants
\textsc{Garden}, which depends on Alex's Cedar and Blair's Maple. Blair's known
preference for \textsc{Harbor} and aversion to \textsc{Workshop} cancel because
those goals have identical requirements; it is neutral toward the remaining
goal. Blair voluntarily offers to commit its own Maple without asking Alex
to commit anything, and Alex accepts. Under the fixed reference continuation,
that offer gives Blair expected terminal utility $0.5$ for each possible
Orchard preference. An offer that also asks Alex to commit Cedar or Maple
would instead give Blair utilities $2$, $1$, and $0.5$ when Blair respectively
\textsc{Want}s, is \textsc{Neutral} toward, or \textsc{Avoid}s Orchard. Thus
the observed offer is suboptimal for \textsc{Want} and \textsc{Neutral}, but
one of three tied optimal offers for \textsc{Avoid}. Its reference-policy
likelihoods are consequently $(0,0,1/3)$; updating the uniform prior leaves
only \textsc{Avoid} possible.

Alex now has the final proposal turn. Offering to add its own Maple while
Blair retains its existing Maple raises Alex's terminal utility from $0$ to
$1.5$: three goals Alex wants each become half complete. Blair's gain from
\textsc{Harbor} cancels its loss from \textsc{Workshop}, so it accepts this
offer on an own-utility tie. Investigating Blair's Orchard preference instead
consumes the last proposal turn and leaves Alex at $0$. The base model
reports all three preferences as possible and none as favored. With that
judgment supplied to a fresh planning call, it investigates; when the same
planner receives the oracle's \textsc{Avoid} judgment, it makes the
$1.5$-utility offer. Asked to act directly from the history, the model
instead proposes that both players add Cedar. An \textsc{Avoid} Blair rejects:
accepting would lower its utility from $0.5$ to $0$, leaving Alex at $0$.
This case exhibits an inference error that the judgment intervention does
repair, although such repair is uncommon across the suite.
