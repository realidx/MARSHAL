\label{sec:diagnose}

We test whether a model can infer a partner's preference ($B$), act under a
correct judgment ($P$), and compose the two.  We freeze legal three-player
histories and intervene on the judgment and first action, which end-game reward
alone cannot do.

\subsection{Controlled native endgames}

We evaluate Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 at temperature zero on 36 positions.  Each
preassigned stratum--split cell contains six distinct source games, with strata
\emph{known}, \emph{unknown--irrelevant}, and \emph{unknown--relevant}.  Ego
knows all but one partner--goal entry, whose
possibilities are \{\textsc{Want}, \textsc{Neutral}, \textsc{Avoid}\}.  We
retain the public history, commitments, schedule, and all legal actions.
Information-limited reference partners compute an exact terminal best response
to a declared continuation.  At most two ego decisions are measured, making
counterfactual enumeration exact.

Selection uses game mechanics, never model answers.  In an unknown--relevant
position, an action optimal for one supported type is strictly suboptimal for
another, certifying $B\!\rightarrow\!P$.  The same position has two legal first
actions whose response branches differ in expected posterior entropy,
certifying $P\!\rightarrow\!B$.  Unknown--irrelevant positions control for
uncertainty that does not change the optimal-action set.  These are task
certificates, not claims about internal neural modules.

For $B$, the model returns every type compatible with history; unresolved sets
are correct.  For $P$, we supply the exact judgment and measure
$\max_{a'}Q_i^{\star}(h,b^{\star},a')-Q_i^{\star}(h,b^{\star},a)$.  We then
force lower- and higher-information actions, enumerate all responses, and test
the next belief, removing the model's first choice as a confound.

\subsection{Both capabilities fail where both are required}

Table~\ref{tab:diagnose-main} pools the two preassigned splits for the 12
unknown--relevant positions.  In all 12, partner information changes both the
best current action and what can be learned next.

The exact support is recovered once in 12 roots; the model averages 0.54 false
exclusions.  Even with the oracle judgment, regret is positive in eight cases
(mean 0.56).  After oracle-selected interactions, the next belief is exact on
only 26.4\% of probability-weighted branches.  The model therefore both
misreads evidence and fails to exploit a correct reading.

\paragraph{Representative failure patterns.}
Three cases make this concrete.  (i) In \texttt{native-30005-g0-d1}, a
partner's offer is compatible with all three types, but the model treats a
commitment used by the goal as a declaration of \textsc{Want} and collapses
the support.  (ii) In the same state, even when given the correct support, it
proposes a two-option offer to $P_1$ worth $0$, while an offer to $P_0$ is worth
$1$: a planning error under an oracle judgment.  (iii) In
\texttt{native-30040-g3-d2}, an informative interaction separates a
\textsc{Want} branch from a \{\textsc{Neutral},\textsc{Avoid}\} branch.  The
model correctly outputs \textsc{Want} after acceptance, yet also outputs it
after the diagnostic rejection, reversing the evidence on that branch.

Known preferences do not eliminate planning error, while a wrong judgment need
not matter when the entry is decision-irrelevant.  Our scoped conclusion is
that both $B$ and $P$ fail in states that couple them, and either failure can
block a higher-value interaction.  This motivates separate supervision of the
two targets and explicit evaluation of their composition.
