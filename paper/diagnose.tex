\label{sec:diagnose}

An unsuccessful interaction does not reveal whether an agent misread its
partner or chose poorly despite an adequate understanding. We diagnose these
two sources of error in the
Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 model~\citep{yang2025qwen3}. At matched decision points,
we ask whether the model can infer a partner's preference from available
evidence, choose a good action given a correct inference, and improve its
choice when an incorrect inference is replaced by an oracle judgment.

\paragraph{Intervening on the inference supplied to planning.}
Each case elicits four responses. The model first reports a preference
judgment $\widehat J_c$ from the history and available private information.
Two separate planning calls then receive the same current state, with either
that judgment ($\mathrm{Plan}_{\mathrm{model}}$) or the oracle judgment $J_c^\star$
($\mathrm{Plan}_{\mathrm{oracle}}$). We also evaluate \emph{direct action selection} from the
original state, without a supplied judgment. The paired planning calls
share their tools, decoding settings, and seed. We remove the behavioral history from both calls so that planning
uses the supplied inference rather than reconstructing it from the original
evidence. To ensure that this removal does not make the task underspecified,
we retain only cases in which the oracle judgment determines the optimal
action set and removing history preserves the continuation payoff table.

\paragraph{Evidence and oracle labels.}
The suite contains 22 two-player cases spanning seven game structures.
Six test use of a preference revealed by investigation; six require ruling
out preferences after one observed partner choice; six retain uncertainty
after one choice; and four require combining successive observations.
Each case has one unknown partner--goal preference, taking values
\textsc{Want}, \textsc{Neutral}, or \textsc{Avoid}. The oracle conditions on
all available evidence under the public prior and reference policy of
Section~\ref{sec:game-slices}. Its label specifies the \emph{support}, the
preferences with positive posterior probability, and the \emph{most likely
preference}. We report the latter only when its posterior probability
exceeds the next highest by more than $0.1$; otherwise, we report
\textsc{Undetermined}. These labels describe what the evidence
supports and we verify
that all posteriors compatible with each oracle label yield the same optimal
action set. Appendix~\ref{app:diagnose} details these constructions.

We report joint inference correctness: a judgment is correct only when both
its support and most likely preference match the oracle label. Action
correctness uses the oracle's acceptable-action set, with the utility
tolerance specified in Appendix~\ref{app:diagnose}. To measure the cost of
an error and the effect of replacing a judgment, let
$V_c(a)=Q_i^\star(I_c,a)$ be the expected terminal utility of action $a$
under the oracle posterior and reference continuation. We report
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{Reg}_c(a)=\max_{a'\in\mathcal L(I_c)}V_c(a')-V_c(a),\qquad
 \Delta_c=V_c\!\left(\widehat a_c(J_c^\star)\right)
          -V_c\!\left(\widehat a_c(\widehat J_c)\right),
 \label{eq:diagnose-regret}
\end{equation}
where $\widehat a_c(J)$ is the model's planning response to judgment $J$.
Both actions are scored under the same posterior and continuation,
$\Delta_c>0$ means that replacing the judgment improves the selected action's
value.

\paragraph{Inference and planning failures.}
The model recovers all six directly revealed preferences but fails all ten
single-choice elimination and multi-step judgments
(Table~\ref{tab:diagnose-main}). Even with oracle inference, planning succeeds
in only $11/22$ cases, with mean regret $0.567$. Correcting an incorrect
judgment changes the action in nine of ten valid pairs: three pairs improve
in value, six preserve it, and one worsens. Across all 21 valid pairs, mean
regret falls from $0.684$ to $0.541$ despite unchanged aggregate action
accuracy. Thus both behavioral inference and action selection exhibit
failures; correcting the supplied inference can improve choices but does
not reliably resolve planning errors. Appendix~\ref{app:diagnose} provides
the breakdown by sensitivity to partner preferences.

\paragraph{An illustrative intervention.}
In Table~\ref{tab:diagnose-example}, Blair asks Alex for a commitment that
makes completing Harbor also complete Orchard. Since Blair could instead
secure Harbor alone, this choice rules out \textsc{Avoid} for Orchard under
the reference policy. The model infers the opposite from Blair's lack of
its own commitment. Replacing that judgment changes its offer from
completing neither goal to completing both, raising utility from $0$ to $2$.
Appendix~\ref{app:diagnose-simple} gives the derivation and recorded outputs;
Appendix~\ref{app:diagnose-example} provides a case where correction leaves
a planning error.

Together, these probes reveal failures in extracting preference information
and in using sufficient information to choose an action. The intervention
connects the two at the explicit judgment--action interface. These findings motivate
training both capabilities alongside direct action selection; the effects
of training and transfer are evaluated separately below.
