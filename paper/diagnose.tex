\label{sec:diagnose}

We ask whether  Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 \citep{qwen2025qwen3instruct} can infer a partner's preference 
from interaction and use that inference to choose a valuable action. 
A poor final decision could
arise because the model misreads its partner, because it chooses poorly even
given a correct reading, or because both steps fail. We therefore examine
the two capabilities at the same decision, and replace the model's
explicit inference answer to test whether its choice depends on that answer.

\paragraph{When an action is evidence.}

We construct 16 such matched two-player structures, eight with binary goals
and eight with linear goals. Within each pair, the current commitments, legal
actions, and payoff of each action under every partner preference are
identical; only the provenance of the displayed behavior changes. The oracle
filters the partner-preference posterior from each history and reports a
qualitative judgment $J_c^\star$: the preferences still possible and, when
one has a sufficiently clear posterior lead, the favored preference. It also
computes the expected terminal value $V_c(a)=Q_i^\star(I_c,a)$ of every
legal action under the fixed reference continuation. We retain only pairs
whose judgments differ, whose optimal-action sets are disjoint, and for
which each qualitative judgment alone determines its optimal-action set.
Thus the information removed from the judgment, cannot explain a planning error on these cases. The label
rule and sufficiency certificate are in Appendix~\ref{app:diagnose}.

\paragraph{Intervening on the judgment.}
For each case $c$, we first ask the model to infer a judgment $\widehat J_c$
from the visible history. We then present the current decision in two fresh
planning calls: one supplies $\widehat J_c$, and the other supplies the
oracle judgment $J_c^\star$. Both calls omit the behavioral history and have everything else unchanged. A
separate call asks the model to choose directly from the history.
We measure decision quality by oracle regret and the paired change in value:
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{Reg}_c(a)=\max_{a'}V_c(a')-V_c(a),\qquad
 \Delta_c=V_c\!\left(\widehat a(J_c^\star)\right)
          -V_c\!\left(\widehat a(\widehat J_c)\right).
 \label{eq:diagnose-regret}
\end{equation}
Here a positive $\Delta_c$ means that supplying the correct judgment
improves the model's chosen action. Both actions in the comparison are
scored under the same true posterior.

\paragraph{Two failures and their interface.}
Table~\ref{tab:diagnose-main} reports the results. The model produces the
exact qualitative judgment in only $3/32$ cases,
with none correct among the 16 voluntary histories. Giving it the correct
judgment still yields an optimal action in only $2/32$ cases (mean regret
$1.151$). Direct choice from the full history yields $2/30$ optimal actions
among valid responses. These results show failures in both evaluated
interfaces on the selected cases.

Replacing the judgment changes the model's action in $19/24$ valid paired
comparisons, so its choice responds to the inference input. Yet only three
replacements improve value, and the mean paired change is $-0.215$. The
matched cases establish that partner inference can
change which action is optimal, while the intervention shows that this model
does not reliably turn a correct judgment into a better choice. This
motivates training the inference-to-planning interface alongside the two
capabilities themselves. The intervention concerns an explicit judgment
passed between calls; it makes no claim about separable internal modules.

\paragraph{An illustrative failure.}
In one retained binary-goal pair (structure \texttt{binary-20358}), the
partner makes an offer that the acting player accepts. The partner's
preference for one goal is hidden. Under the reference policy, the likelihood
of that offer is $0$, $0$, and $0.2$ if the partner respectively
\textsc{Want}s, is \textsc{Neutral} toward, or \textsc{Avoid}s the goal.
Thus a voluntary offer identifies \textsc{Avoid}; when the identical offer
is imposed by the setup, it leaves the uniform prior unchanged. The model
nevertheless reports all three preferences as possible, with none favored,
in the voluntary case. Given its own judgment, it selects a new offer worth
$2$, which is optimal under the true posterior. Replacing the judgment
with the oracle's \textsc{Avoid} makes the same model planner \textsc{pass},
worth only $1$. The correction changes the action but worsens it: the model
both misses the behavioral evidence and fails to use the correct inference
reliably when it is supplied.
