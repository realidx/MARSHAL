\section{CalBench evaluation and rollout comparison}
\label{app:calbench}

\subsection{Environment and evaluation settings}
\label{app:calbench-setting}

\paragraph{Task and private information.}
CalBench~\citep{zou2026calbench} models personal assistants coordinating a
stream of meeting requests on behalf of different users. Each agent controls
one private calendar with discrete time slots. A slot can be free, contain a
movable errand or a previously scheduled meeting, or be blocked and therefore
unavailable for either scheduling or rescheduling. The agent sees its own
calendar, item-specific displacement costs, the current meeting request, and
messages delivered to it; it cannot inspect another agent's calendar.
Agents are instructed to minimize their own user's cumulative displacement
cost while coordinating feasible meetings and avoiding disclosure of exact
costs or unnecessary private details. Thus a mutually feasible slot may
require one user to move an errand or several users to move a shared meeting,
creating a need to negotiate who accommodates whom. Scheduling a new meeting
itself incurs no displacement cost; moving an existing item does.

\paragraph{One meeting round.}
Each incoming request identifies a meeting and its participants. The calendar
state persists across rounds, and a verbal agreement alone does not change
it. Our evaluation retains the native four-phase interaction:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Communication.} Agents exchange private direct messages to
propose slots, explain constraints, and negotiate calendar changes. A
participant may also contact an agent outside the current meeting when an
earlier shared commitment needs to move.
\item \textbf{Voluntary rescheduling.} A non-participant who received a
message during the round can submit changes to its own calendar. This phase
allows an earlier meeting's participants to help relocate it even when they
are absent from the new meeting.
\item \textbf{Independent decisions.} Each current participant submits a
batch of \texttt{schedule} and \texttt{reschedule} actions for its own calendar.
A reschedule identifies the item, its source and destination slots, and a
justification. The batch is validated as a whole: the new meeting must occupy
a free slot after the proposed moves, and blocked items cannot be moved or
overwritten. No agent can directly edit a partner's calendar.
\item \textbf{Resolution.} The environment validates the submitted actions
and checks agreement across calendars. All participants must place the new
meeting in the same slot, and any relocated meeting must remain consistent
across its original participants. Agreement in messages is therefore
insufficient if the agents submit incompatible calendar actions.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Our sequential evaluation suite.}
We evaluate homogeneous teams: the same model controls all four agents in a
game. Each game has eight slots and three single-slot meetings, with
participant sets $\{0,1,2\}$, $\{1,2,3\}$, and $\{0,2,3\}$ in that order.
There are no preloaded meetings; shared commitments arise from the agents'
own earlier decisions. Future requests and evaluator reference solutions
are withheld from agents. The frozen suite crosses four conditions with two
errand-cost regimes and three scenario seeds, giving $4\times2\times3=24$
games:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Loose availability:} generator density $0.6$, with movable
errands and no blocked items, tests scheduling with spare calendar capacity.
\item \textbf{Dense calendars:} generator density $1.0$, with no blocked
items, increases the need to accommodate existing commitments. Density is
applied to residual capacity after reserved slots, rather than to the raw
fraction of occupied slots.
\item \textbf{Blocked errands:} density $1.0$ and two immovable items per
agent combine hard availability constraints with movable errands.
\item \textbf{Replanning control:} an authored calendar configuration makes
an earlier meeting's placement consequential for a later request. Placing
the first meeting at slot 0 requires moving it to slot 1 when the second
request arrives, including contacting agent 0, who is absent from that
request. Placing it at slot 1 initially avoids this branch; successful
completion does not by itself demonstrate replanning.
\end{itemize}
Movable errands cost 1 to relocate in the uniform regime and have integer
costs from 1 to 8 in the varied regime. Moving an existing meeting costs 1
per participant. The three scenario seeds change private occupancy and
costs, rather than merely repeating stochastic model decoding. The
replanning controls admit a zero-cost solution in both cost regimes.
All cases are verified as feasible by reference schedules and native
sequential replay. This suite is a small-model adaptation of CalBench; we
report coordination outcomes rather than reproduce its original aggregate
benchmark score. Although the native prompts retain privacy instructions,
we do not measure privacy leakage, and message volume is used only as a
communication metric.

\paragraph{Inference settings and comparison models.}
All models use the same cases, direct messages only, temperature zero, a
4,096-token output limit, and a 32,768-token context limit. Each meeting
allows four communication turns per participant and one decision retry;
fallback and reflection are disabled. The Qwen3-4B thinking model, MARSHAL
Generalist~\citep{yuan2026marshal}, and Social-R1-4B~\citep{wu2026socialr1}
form a separate comparison group because they use thinking-mode checkpoints;
\emph{Baseline} denotes the unchanged Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 model, and
\emph{Self-play} denotes its complete-game self-play-trained counterpart.
The slice-trained variants are \textsc{GameSlice-A} (direct action selection),
\textsc{GameSlice-AP} (direct action selection and conditional planning), and
\textsc{GameSlice-Joint} (all three objectives), initialized from the same
instruct model. These names match Table~\ref{tab:experiment-calbench}.

\paragraph{Metric definitions.}
A meeting is retained only if it remains in one common slot for all
participants in the final calendars. A game is successful only if all three
meetings remain valid and blocked items are preserved. These counts have
maxima of 72 meetings and 24 games. For a successful game $g$, per-agent
excess cost is $(C_g-C_g^\star)/4$, where $C_g$ is the realized team
rescheduling cost and $C_g^\star$ is the minimum full-stream cost, established
by the lower-bound and attainability argument below. We average this quantity over successful games only, so
models may be evaluated on different subsets of games. The cost reference
has access to the full scenario after the fact and is not an online policy.

\paragraph{Minimum-cost reference.}
Every pair of meetings shares a participant, so their final slots must be
distinct. We enumerate all $8\cdot7\cdot6=336$ assignments of the three
meetings to distinct slots, excluding assignments that occupy a participant's
blocked slot. For each remaining assignment, the sum of the displacement
costs of initial errands occupying its meeting slots is a lower bound:
each such errand must move at least once. Minimizing over these assignments
therefore gives a lower bound on every successful execution. For every
evaluation scenario, a native sequential execution attains this bound,
establishing $C_g^\star$. Replay proves attainability; enumeration supplies
the lower bound needed for optimality. This reference uses all three
requests in hindsight, whereas agents receive them sequentially.

\paragraph{Communication overhead.}
Let $M_{ig}$ be the number of direct messages sent by agent $i$ throughout
game $g$, including messages during unsuccessful rounds and messages sent
as a non-participant. Received messages are not counted. Let $R_{ig}$ be
the number of meeting rounds in which $i$ participates and the round
resolution declares the meeting successfully coordinated. These are
round-level successes, not finally retained meetings: a later inconsistency
does not remove an earlier success from this denominator. We report
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{Comm}=\frac{1}{24}\sum_{g=1}^{24}\frac{1}{4}
 \sum_{i=1}^{4}\frac{M_{ig}}{\max(R_{ig},1)}.
\end{equation}
When $R_{ig}=0$, the agent contributes its full sent-message count, including
zero if it sent nothing. All games enter this average, regardless of final
success.

\paragraph{Burden imbalance.}
Let $S_g$ contain meetings declared coordinated at their respective round
resolutions, and let $c_{ig}$ be agent $i$'s total realized displacement cost
over the entire game, including costs incurred during unsuccessful rounds.
The native assignment solver is run on the initial calendars for $S_g$.
Its selected reference assignment is replayed in request order to obtain
per-agent reference costs $r_{ig}(S_g)$. This is the cost allocation of the
selected team-cost reference, not each agent's independently minimized cost
or an equal share of the team cost. Define
\begin{equation}
 b_{ig}=c_{ig}-r_{ig}(S_g),\qquad
 \bar b_g=\frac14\sum_{i=1}^{4}b_{ig},\qquad
 \mathrm{Imbalance}=\frac1{24}\sum_{g=1}^{24}\frac14
 \sum_{i=1}^{4}|b_{ig}-\bar b_g|.
\end{equation}
Individual excess burdens may be negative and are not clipped. Failed games
remain in the average with their realized costs and their own $S_g$;
if $S_g$ is empty, all reference costs are zero. The implementation also
uses zero reference costs if the selected assignment cannot be replayed.
Thus imbalance uses a round-success-subset reference, whereas the reported
excess-cost column uses the full-stream reference and successful games only.

\subsection{Rollout comparison with verbatim model outputs}
\label{app:calbench-rollout}

The following excerpts come from the frozen \texttt{replan\_varied\_s1}
scenario. Baseline, Self-play, and \textsc{GameSlice-Joint} received
identical initial calendars. Slot tuples below come from the environment's round resolutions,
ordered by the meeting's participants, rather than from model explanations.

\paragraph{Negotiating the first meeting.}
\textsc{GameSlice-Joint}'s agent 0 first proposed slot 2. Agent 1 replied:
\begin{quote}\small
``Hi, slot 2 is blocked for me, so I can't use it. Slot 1 is free for me and
would be a good alternative. Would that work for you?''
\end{quote}
Agent 0 then asked agent 2:
\begin{quote}\small
``Hi, Agent 1 suggested slot 1. Is that free for you? I'm open to slot 1 if it
works for both of you.''
\end{quote}
Agent 2 confirmed slot 1, and the three agents' scheduling actions all chose
slot 1; the resolution tuple was $(1,1,1)$.

\paragraph{A disagreement in the other runs.}
Baseline and Self-play both placed the first meeting at slot 0. During negotiation for
the second meeting, Baseline's agent 3 reported to agent 1:
\begin{quote}\small
``Slot 1 is blocked for me, so I can't use it. How about slot 2? It's free and
doesn't conflict with my schedule. Would that work for you?''
\end{quote}
Baseline's agent 1 later responded:
\begin{quote}\small
``I see slot 1 is blocked for you, but it's free for me. I don't have any other
free slots available. Could you please confirm if you're able to adjust your
schedule to slot 1? I'm happy to help coordinate if needed, but we need a
shared free slot.''
\end{quote}
Self-play's agent 1 made the corresponding request to agent 3:
\begin{quote}\small
``I see you're suggesting slot 2, but it's blocked for me and cannot be used.
Slot 1 is free and available for me without any conflict. Would you be able to
join us there? If not, let me know what alternative works best for you.''
\end{quote}
In both runs, the second
meeting's resolution tuple was $(1,2,2)$, so no common slot was established.
Baseline's agent 1 actually returned an empty action list at this decision; its
resolution entry should not be read as an intentional slot-1 commitment.

\paragraph{Preserving a shared commitment.}
In the \textsc{GameSlice-Joint} run, the second meeting was placed at slot 0
by all three participants. When agent 0 proposed slot 0 for the third
meeting, agent 2 replied:
\begin{quote}\small
``Slot 0 is taken by another meeting. I suggest slot 2 instead, which is free
and doesn't conflict with any of my commitments. Would that work for you?''
\end{quote}
Agent 3 also identified the prior meeting at slot 0. The final resolution
tuple was $(2,2,2)$, and all three meetings remained valid. This trajectory
shows coordination around an existing commitment; it contains no action that
reschedules an earlier meeting.
