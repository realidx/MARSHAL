\subsection{Multi-Agent Coordination as Belief-Conditioned Sequential Decision Making}

In sequential multi-agent decision making, the consequence of an ego action
depends not only on the physical state but also on how other agents respond.
This dependence is particularly important when a partner's goals, incentives,
or other behaviorally relevant information are not directly observable. We
therefore adopt the interactive POMDP (I-POMDP) perspective, which incorporates
models of other agents into sequential decision making and maps beliefs over
interactive states to actions~\citep{gmytrasiewicz2005ipomdp}.

Following the canonical definition of Gmytrasiewicz and Doshi, an I-POMDP for
ego agent $i$ is

\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{I\mbox{-}POMDP}_i
 =\left\langle IS_i,A,T_i,\Omega_i,O_i,R_i\right\rangle,
 \qquad
 IS_i=S\times\prod_{j\ne i}M_j,
 \qquad
 A=\prod_{j=1}^{N}A_j,
 \label{eq:ipomdp-definition}
\end{equation}

where $S$ is the physical state space, $M_j$ is the set of possible models of
partner $j$, and $A$ is the joint action space. In the original two-agent
presentation, $IS_i=S\times M_j$ and $A=A_i\times A_j$; the product form above
is its direct multi-agent generalization. Each model $m_j\in M_j$ is described
by $m_j=\langle h_j,f_j,O_j\rangle$, where $h_j$ is a possible observation
history, $f_j:H_j\rightarrow\Delta(A_j)$ maps that history to a distribution
over $j$'s actions, and $O_j$ specifies the input supplied to $j$ by the
environment. Models may be subintentional, $M_j^{\mathrm{S}}$, or intentional,
$M_j^{\mathrm{I}}$, so that $M_j=M_j^{\mathrm{I}}\cup M_j^{\mathrm{S}}$. An
intentional model is a type $\theta_j=\langle b_j,\widehat\theta_j\rangle$,
where $b_j$ is $j$'s belief over the physical state and other-agent models and
$\widehat\theta_j$ is its frame, including its action, observation, transition,
reward, and optimality specifications. Thus, the recursive belief structure is
part of the standard model space rather than an additional assumption introduced
here~\citep{gmytrasiewicz2005ipomdp}.

The remaining components are the transition, observation, and reward models,

\begin{equation}
 T_i:S\times A\times S\rightarrow[0,1],
 \qquad
 O_i:S\times A\times\Omega_i\rightarrow[0,1],
 \qquad
 R_i:IS_i\times A\rightarrow\mathbb R.
 \label{eq:ipomdp-components}
\end{equation}

The original formulation imposes model non-manipulability (MNM): an agent's
actions do not directly modify another agent's model, and model
non-observability (MNO): an agent cannot directly observe another agent's model.
Consequently, an agent maintains a belief $b_t^i\in\Delta(IS_i)$ over
interactive states and updates it from its own action and observation. Using the
belief-state estimation operator from the original formulation, this update is

\begin{equation}
 b_{t+1}^i
 =\mathrm{SE}_{\theta_i}\!\left(b_t^i,a_t^i,o_{t+1}^i\right)
 \;\equiv\;
 \tau_i\!\left(b_t^i,a_t^i,o_{t+1}^i\right).
 \label{eq:ipomdp-belief-update}
\end{equation}

At this level, $\mathrm{SE}_{\theta_i}$ is not a filter over a static partner
label: for an intentional model, the original update marginalizes over partner
actions and observations, the physical transition, and the partner's own belief
update. This recursive dependence is precisely what distinguishes an
interactive belief from a flat POMDP state belief.

For an intentional partner model, the expected immediate reward in the value
recursion is averaged over the partner actions predicted by its model. In the
two-agent definition, this quantity is

\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{ER}_i(x,a_i)
 =\sum_{a_j\in A_j}R_i\!\left(x,(a_i,a_j)\right)
 \Pr(a_j\mid m_j),
 \label{eq:ipomdp-expected-reward}
\end{equation}

and is replaced by an expectation over the joint action $a_{-i}$ for multiple
partners. The canonical I-POMDP solution maps the agent's type to an action through

\begin{equation}
 \begin{split}
 U_i(\theta_i)=\max_{a_i\in A_i}\Big[&
 \sum_{x\in IS_i}b_i(x)\,\operatorname{ER}_i(x,a_i) \\
 &+\gamma\sum_{o_i\in\Omega_i}
 \Pr(o_i\mid a_i,b_i)\,
 U_i\!\left(\left\langle
 \mathrm{SE}_{\theta_i}(b_i,a_i,o_i),\widehat\theta_i
 \right\rangle\right)\Big].
 \end{split}
 \label{eq:ipomdp-value-recursion}
\end{equation}

Here $\Pr(o_i\mid a_i,b_i)$ is induced by $T_i$, $O_i$, and the models of the
other agents. The belief update and value recursion above are the standard
I-POMDP mechanisms: the former updates a distribution over interactive states,
while the latter selects an ego action after integrating over the predicted
behavior of other agents. For the finite-horizon environment used here, we
instantiate the same dependency with undiscounted terminal utility and no
separately shaped intermediate reward.

The two terms below are therefore not additional state variables or a new
coordination formalism. They are functional views of the standard interactive
belief and solution mapping. For the sequential notation used below, $s_t$
denotes the public interaction state, $a_t^i$ an ego action,
$r_{t+1}^j$ a response emitted by partner $j$ when an interaction elicits one,
$o_t^i$ the observation available to ego $i$, and $e_{t+1}^i$ the evidence
extracted from the next observed interaction.

A \emph{partner belief} is the ego's current belief about the partner
components of the interactive state: equivalently, the uncertainty over the
partners' models or types that is relevant to predicting their future behavior.
In the original I-POMDP, this object is represented within $b_t^i$ and can
include partners' private preferences, capabilities, observations, and beliefs.
In an implementation, its representation may be a finite support, a structured
state, or an implicit internal representation; it need not be exposed as an
explicit probability vector or required as a model output. This use of a
behavioral model revised from observed input--output behavior is also standard
in opponent modeling~\citep{carmel1996opponent}.

\emph{Partner-conditioned planning} is the complementary use of that belief in
the action-selection part of the I-POMDP solution. Given $b_t^i$, the ego uses
the models in the support of its belief to predict partner responses, e.g.,
$r_{t+1}^j\sim\widehat\pi_j^{\,i}(\cdot\mid s_t,a_t^i,b_t^i)$, evaluates
the resulting downstream utility, and selects its own action. The candidate
action may determine which partner to engage and what interaction to propose;
when the ego is responding, the response itself is its action. This functional
view is consistent with prior work that couples inferred partner goals with
goal-conditioned behavior models and planning~\citep{huang2024hop}.

The two objects are distinct but coupled. The current belief determines the
partner model used in value evaluation, while the selected action affects which
partner behaviors and outcomes are likely to be observed. The action therefore
changes the distribution of future evidence, not the belief-update rule itself:
the next belief is still obtained by applying $\tau_i$ to the evidence that
becomes available. Functionally, the loop is

\begin{equation}
 b_t^i
 \;\longrightarrow\;
 \underbrace{\mathsf P_t^i=\mathsf{Model}(b_t^i)}_{\text{partner model}}
 \;\longrightarrow\;
 a_t^i
 \;\longrightarrow\;
 e_{t+1}^i
 \;\longrightarrow\;
 b_{t+1}^i
 \;\longrightarrow\;
 \mathsf P_{t+1}^i .
 \label{eq:belief-planning-loop}
\end{equation}

This is a functional decomposition for analysis, not an architectural
requirement: the two objects need not be implemented as independent modules,
and the equations alone do not assert a capability deficit in any particular
agent.

\subsection{BENAC-P: Negotiation with Private Preferences and Binding Commitments}
\label{sec:benac-p}

We introduce BENAC-P, a multi-player negotiation game in which agents negotiate
binding commitments toward shared public goals while holding private preferences
over those goals. Building on BENAC's commitment-based negotiation
structure~\citep{benac2026benchmark}, we make partner incentives hidden and allow
agents to propose menus of alternative agreements. A partner's response both
changes the feasible continuation and provides evidence about its preferences.
This gives a concrete setting for the belief--planning loop above.

\paragraph{Game specification.}
A game is specified by
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal M=
 \left\langle\mathcal N,\mathbf K,\mathcal G,\Gamma,
 \rho,C_0,\mathcal L,\mu_0\right\rangle.
 \label{eq:benac-game}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal N=\{0,\ldots,N-1\}$ is the player set, with $N\geq3$ in our
experiments, and $\mathbf K=(K_i)_{i\in\mathcal N}$ gives each player's number
of binary commitments. The set $\mathcal G=\{0,\ldots,G-1\}$ indexes public
goals; $\Gamma=(\Gamma_g)_{g\in\mathcal G}$ specifies the commitments required
by each goal. The schedule $\rho=(\rho_0,\ldots,\rho_{T-1})$ identifies the
proposer at each turn, $C_0$ is the initial commitment state, and
$\mathcal L$ specifies the legal proposals at each state. Finally, $\mu_0$
is the publicly specified distribution over private preference configurations.
All components of $\mathcal M$ are public; the realized preferences are not.

\paragraph{Commitments, preferences, and utility.}
At turn $t$, player $i$ has commitments $c_{t,i}\in\{0,1\}^{K_i}$, and
$C_t=(c_{t,i})_{i\in\mathcal N}$ is the joint commitment state. An entry
$c_{t,i,k}=1$ records an irreversible commitment. Each requirement set
$\Gamma_g\subseteq\{(i,k):i\in\mathcal N,\ 1\leq k\leq K_i\}$ involves at
least two players. A goal is satisfied exactly when all its requirements hold:
\begin{equation}
 S_g(C_t)=\mathbf 1\!\left[
 c_{t,i,k}=1\ \text{for every }(i,k)\in\Gamma_g\right].
 \label{eq:goal-satisfaction}
\end{equation}
At initialization, the environment selects a preference matrix
$V=(v_{i,g})\sim\mu_0$, where
$v_{i,g}\in\{-1,0,+1\}$ represents \textsc{Avoid}, \textsc{Neutral}, and
\textsc{Want}, respectively. Player $i$ observes its own row $v_i$; other rows
remain private, although the public configuration distribution can correlate
them. The episode ends after the scheduled interactions, and player $i$ receives
\begin{equation}
 U_i(C_T;v_i)=\sum_{g\in\mathcal G}v_{i,g}S_g(C_T).
 \label{eq:terminal-utility}
\end{equation}
There is no separately shaped intermediate reward. Agents therefore share the
goal structure without necessarily sharing objectives: completing the same goal
can benefit one player and harm another.

\paragraph{Offers, responses, and transitions.}
At turn $t$, proposer $p=\rho_t$ chooses a proposal from $\mathcal L(s_t)$,
where $s_t=(C_t,t,h_t)$ contains the commitments, turn index, and public
transcript. An ordinary offer to partner $q\ne p$ is
$o=(q,\bar c_p,\bar c_q)$, specifying the two players' target commitment
vectors. Targets must preserve all existing commitments, add at most $M$ new
commitments per side, add no action in a public forbidden set $F$, and add at
least one commitment in total. These constraints, together with any public
stage-specific proposal restrictions, define $\mathcal L$.

The responder chooses \textsc{Accept} or \textsc{Reject}. Acceptance makes the
proposed commitments immediately binding:
\begin{equation}
 c_{t+1,i}=
 \begin{cases}
 c_{t,i}\lor\bar c_i, & i\in\{p,q\},\\
 c_{t,i}, & \text{otherwise}.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:offer-transition}
\end{equation}
Rejection leaves $C_t$ unchanged. A menu $m=(o^{(1)},o^{(2)})$ contains two
distinct legal offers to the same responder, who chooses
\textsc{Choose\_1}, \textsc{Choose\_2}, or \textsc{Reject}. Only the selected
offer binds; rejection binds neither. Where allowed, \textsc{Pass} skips the
proposal and leaves commitments unchanged. Each resolved interaction advances
the schedule by one turn, and the proposal, response, and resulting state are
publicly observed. A pending offer is hypothetical until accepted.

Although each agreement is bilateral, its commitments can enable or constrain
later agreements with other players. A menu also lets ego choose which
preference comparisons a partner faces. Information is consequently obtained
through ordinary negotiation actions that have real task consequences, rather
than through a separate preference-query action.

\paragraph{Deterministic rational partners.}
To diagnose ego behavior under a known response model, we use deterministic
partners in the controlled subgames of Section~\ref{sec:diagnose}. For a
proposal $a$ addressed to $j$, let $\mathcal R(a)$ be its legal response set
and let $\mathsf T(s,a,r)$ be the public transition defined above. The partner
compares responses using only public commitments and its own preferences:
\begin{equation}
 f_j(s,a;v_j)\in\arg\max_{r\in\mathcal R(a)}
 \sum_{g\in\mathcal G}v_{j,g}
 S_g\!\left(C\bigl(\mathsf T(s,a,r)\bigr)\right),
 \label{eq:oracle-partner}
\end{equation}
where $C(s)$ extracts the commitment state. Rejection wins ties with an
agreement; among equally valuable accepted menu options, the earlier option
wins. We certify each subgame by enumerating allowed continuations and verifying
that this commitment-state ranking also maximizes the responder's terminal
utility. Thus, the snapshot comparison is terminal-rational in the certified
family; it is not assumed to be sufficient in arbitrary negotiation games.
The acting partner never reads another player's private preference row.
Uncertainty comes from hidden preferences, not randomized partner behavior.

\paragraph{Exact belief update and reference planning.}
For ego $i$, a hypothesis $h=(v_j)_{j\ne i}$ is a joint configuration of partner
preferences. The solver initializes a distribution $b_0^i$ by conditioning
$\mu_0$ on ego's own row and any supplied initial information. Its support
$\mathcal H_t^i$ retains cross-partner correlations. After ego proposes $a$ and
observes response $r$ from $j$, the exact update is
\begin{equation}
 b_{t+1}^i(h)=
 \frac{b_t^i(h)\,\mathbf 1[f_j(s_t,a;v_j(h))=r]}
 {\sum_{h'\in\mathcal H_t^i}
 b_t^i(h')\,\mathbf 1[f_j(s_t,a;v_j(h'))=r]}.
 \label{eq:bayes-filter}
\end{equation}
Inconsistent configurations are eliminated; ego's proposal itself is an
intervention, not evidence about hidden preferences. Our diagnostic population
is uniform over its compatible configurations, so this update reduces to exact
support filtering. The model-facing belief can therefore be expressed as a
semantic set without requiring numerical probabilities.

The reference planner enumerates the remaining interaction tree. In our
diagnostic subgames, ego makes the scheduled proposals and partners supply the
responses, including the final approval. Define
$p_b(r\mid s,a)=\sum_h b(h)\mathbf 1[f_j(s,a;v_j(h))=r]$ and let
$\tau(b,s,a,r)$ denote the update above. The solver computes
\begin{equation}
 W_i(s,b)=\max_{a\in\mathcal L(s)}
 \sum_{r\in\mathcal R(a)}p_b(r\mid s,a)\,
 W_i\!\left(\mathsf T(s,a,r),\tau(b,s,a,r)\right),
 \qquad W_i(s_T,b)=U_i(C_T;v_i),
 \label{eq:benac-reference-planner}
\end{equation}
with zero-probability branches omitted. A pass has one null response and leaves
belief unchanged; an automatic protocol step has a singleton action set.
This solver selects actions by terminal utility after observation, updating,
and replanning. It provides exact judgments and reference actions for diagnosis
under the specified partner policies, without giving ego access to the realized
hidden configuration or requiring an equilibrium solution.
