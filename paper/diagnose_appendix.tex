\section{Diagnostic protocol and detailed results}
\label{app:diagnose}

\paragraph{Cases and evidence.}
The main diagnostic uses the frozen v9 suite of 22 two-player cases spanning
seven distinct game geometries. Its 12 basic cases comprise six direct
feedback, three single-choice elimination, and three ambiguous single-choice
cases, with two or three binary goals per basic case. Ten challenge cases
comprise three additional elimination cases, three additional ambiguous
cases, and four multi-step updates. Cases sharing a geometry are related
probes rather than independent draws from a task population. The structures
were reused during diagnostic development; this is not an untouched transfer
benchmark. The v9 selection procedure uses structural and oracle criteria,
not model success on the selected cases.

Initial commitments are declared starting conditions, not fictitious partner
choices. Direct feedback comes from a legal focal-player investigation with
a truthful private answer. That query is an intervention and need not be
optimal; it does not supply behavioral evidence about the partner. Other
cases use observed choices with positive probability under the specified
partner policy. Labels condition on public facts, own preferences, private
observations, and the relevant behavioral evidence.

\paragraph{Judgments and planning sufficiency.}
Each case queries exactly one unknown partner--goal preference, with
candidate values \textsc{Want}, \textsc{Neutral}, and \textsc{Avoid}.
The oracle judgment contains its positive-probability support and the most
likely preference. We report the latter only when its posterior probability
exceeds the runner-up by more than $0.1$; otherwise, we report
\textsc{Undetermined}. This label does not imply equal posterior probabilities.
We report joint correctness,
requiring both components to match the oracle label. All labels are relative to the public
prior and specified reference policy.

Planning uses the standard history-free training renderer, including tables
of goal requirements and preferences and qualitative supplied beliefs.
Behavioral history and numerical posterior probabilities are omitted.
For each oracle judgment, exact rational polytope-vertex checks verify that
all compatible posteriors induce the same optimal-action set. The admission
checks also verify the scorer's tolerance-based acceptable-action set and
recompute continuation payoffs from the current state without the removed
history. Every retained case passes these checks. Correctness follows the
certified acceptable-action set; regret uses the exact expected-utility gap.
The scorer allows an own-utility gap of at most $0.1$ from the best action.
At offer responses, it additionally allows a gap of at most $0.1$ in the
other players' total utility relative to actions tied in own utility.
The oracle policy itself uses exact utility maximization and the response
tie rule; these scoring tolerances do not alter that policy.

For each possible partner preference, the qualification procedure computes
the acceptable-action set under the same per-world payoff table. A case is
repair-sensitive if at least one such set is disjoint from the set accepted
under the oracle judgment. This is a structural criterion involving an
alternative preference, not a guarantee that the model's actual judgment
error requires a different action. The remaining cases are labeled action
controls in the archive. This label denotes the complement of the
repair-sensitive group, not a negative control for the model's response to
its input. There are nine repair-sensitive cases and thirteen controls.
All four multi-step cases are controls and each admits an acceptable action
shared across all three preferences; they therefore do not require a
belief-dependent change of action.

\paragraph{Requests and execution.}
Every case has four calls: inference, direct action selection,
$\mathrm{Plan}_{\mathrm{oracle}}$, and $\mathrm{Plan}_{\mathrm{model}}$. The archive denotes the first two by
$B$ and $O$, respectively.
The two planning requests share the current state, tools, decoding settings,
and request seed; only the supplied judgment differs. Inference requests require a
brief explanation followed by one belief-submission tool call, with explicit
set semantics. Action calls use the native action tools. The intervention
changes an explicit input between fresh calls rather than manipulating an
internal model representation.

The reported run evaluates Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 once per case, at temperature
zero, top-$p=1$, top-$k=-1$, and a 4,096-token completion limit. The service
uses vLLM 0.28.0, BF16, one H100 GPU, batch-invariant mode, prefix caching,
and eager execution; chunked prefill and cascade attention are disabled.
Runner concurrency is four and the run seed is 42. All 88 calls returned,
with no truncation or infrastructure failure. One $\mathrm{Plan}_{\mathrm{model}}$ call has a
format failure. It counts as incorrect for accuracy and is excluded from
paired utility comparisons, leaving 21 valid pairs. No repeated-run
uncertainty estimate is claimed.

\paragraph{Aggregation and intervention accounting.}
The repair-sensitive and control panels are reported separately. Overall
counts are descriptive: joint inference correctness is $11/22$;
direct action selection, $\mathrm{Plan}_{\mathrm{oracle}}$, and
$\mathrm{Plan}_{\mathrm{model}}$ are each correct in $11/22$. Basic-case joint inference and $\mathrm{Plan}_{\mathrm{oracle}}$ correctness
are $9/12$ and $7/12$, versus $2/10$ and $4/10$ on challenge cases.

Across 21 valid pairs, three replacements increase utility, seventeen leave
it unchanged, and one reduces it. Among the ten pairs with a changed
judgment, these counts are three, six, and one, respectively. There are nine
action changes, including five that preserve utility. All eleven pairs with
unchanged judgments reproduce the same action. Mean paired regret falls
from $0.684$ to $0.541$, a value gain of $0.143$. The all-case
$\mathrm{Plan}_{\mathrm{oracle}}$ mean regret is $0.567$ and uses a different denominator.
For each pair, the value gain equals model-judgment regret minus
oracle-judgment regret under the same scoring posterior. These effects can
have either sign and are not an additive decomposition of the direct
action-selection error.

The repair-sensitive panel has one positive and seven zero gains among its
eight valid pairs. The control panel has two positive, ten zero, and one
negative gain among thirteen pairs. In the single-choice elimination layer,
mean repair gain is $0.667$ across six cases, but two of its three positive
gains occur in controls. This distinction prevents improvements in controls
from being presented as evidence that the task requires preference-dependent
action selection.
Of the eight valid repair-sensitive pairs, five already have the correct
judgment. Among the three with an incorrect judgment, two actions change;
one gains $1$ in utility and two have zero gain. The mean gain is therefore
$0.333$ over corrected judgments and $0.125$ over all valid pairs in this
group. In the control group, seven judgments are incorrect; all seven
actions change, with two gains, four ties, and one loss. The corresponding
means are $0.286$ and $0.154$ over corrected judgments and all thirteen
valid pairs, respectively.

\subsection{Two-goal example: ruling out an avoided goal}
\label{app:diagnose-simple}

Table~\ref{tab:diagnose-example} uses the archived case
\texttt{layer-19646c73e0c97ae729}. Starting with no commitments, Blair acts
first and Alex acts last; neither has an investigation use remaining.
The prior over Blair's Orchard preference is uniform. Recomputing the
reference policy gives likelihoods $(1/6,1/9,0)$ for the observed offer
under \textsc{Want}, \textsc{Neutral}, and \textsc{Avoid}. Alex accepts
with probability one, so the posterior is $(0.6,0.4,0)$ and the oracle
judgment is Want or Neutral, with Want most likely. Excluding Avoid has the
simple payoff argument given in the main text; the unequal weights on the
two remaining preferences follow from the reference policy's uniform
mixing over tied actions.

The model instead states, ``Blair's behavior (not adding Cedar) suggests
they do not want Orchard,'' and submits only \texttt{avoid}. Its planning
call requests \texttt{self\_commitments=[]} and
\texttt{partner\_commitments=[Maple]}, completing no goal regardless of
Blair's response. With the oracle judgment, the call requests
\texttt{self\_commitments=[Maple]} and
\texttt{partner\_commitments=[Cedar]}. Blair accepts, completing both goals
and giving Alex $2$. The archived action indices are 2 and 4, respectively,
with regrets $2$ and $0$ and intervention gain $2$.
The latter offer is also acceptable if Blair avoids Orchard: its own
utility then ties rejection at $0$, and the response tie rule favors
Alex's gain. This explains the case's action-control classification and
limits the conclusion to the observed benefit of correcting the model's
supplied judgment.

\subsection{A policy-dependent example of partial correction}

\label{app:diagnose-example}

This additional example is archived as
\texttt{interaction-0524ffa3f232ff556a}. Its two proposal opportunities are
Blair followed by Alex, starting with no binding commitments. Each offer can
add at most one commitment per player, and commitments cannot be removed.
All four goals are binary, with requirements and preferences as follows.
Only Blair's preference for Orchard is hidden; its prior is uniform over
\textsc{Want}, \textsc{Neutral}, and \textsc{Avoid}.
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lllll}
\hline
Goal & Alex must add & Blair must add & Alex's preference & Blair's preference \\
\hline
Orchard & Maple & Maple & Want & Hidden \\
Harbor & Cedar & Maple & Want & Want \\
Library & Maple & Maple & Neutral & Avoid \\
Garden & Cedar & Cedar & Avoid & Avoid \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Observed history and oracle inference.}
Blair proposes no commitment for itself and Cedar for Alex; Alex accepts.
To evaluate this choice as evidence, the oracle compares its continuation
value with the other actions Blair could have taken. Under the selected
reference policy, Alex's subsequent proposal adds Maple for both players.
If Blair wants Orchard, accepting completes Orchard, Harbor, and Library,
giving Blair $1+1-1=1$. If it is neutral, accepting gives $0+1-1=0$;
rejection also gives $0$, so the response tie rule favors acceptance for
Alex's benefit. If Blair avoids Orchard, accepting gives $-1+1-1=-1$,
so it rejects and receives $0$.

Blair has an alternative first offer: add Maple for itself and Cedar for
Alex. Alex accepts, immediately completing Harbor. On Alex's last turn,
adding its Maple would also complete Orchard and Library. Blair accepts
this addition if it wants Orchard and otherwise rejects it. Thus the
alternative secures Blair terminal utility $1$ under every preference.
The observed offer is optimal only under \textsc{Want}, where all thirteen
legal first actions tie and the reference policy mixes uniformly. Its
likelihoods are therefore
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \Pr(\text{observed offer}\mid\textsc{Want})&=\tfrac1{13},\\
 \Pr(\text{observed offer}\mid\textsc{Neutral})
 =\Pr(\text{observed offer}\mid\textsc{Avoid})&=0.
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Alex accepts the observed offer with probability one. Bayes' rule maps the
prior $(1/3,1/3,1/3)$ to posterior $(1,0,0)$, yielding support
$\{\textsc{Want}\}$ and most likely preference \textsc{Want}. This inference follows
from the stated reference policy and continuation, rather than from a
general assumption that any offer reveals a unique preference.

\paragraph{Recorded model responses and their consequences.}
The model's inference response instead states, ``There is no evidence
suggesting Blair wants or avoids Orchard,'' and submits all three values
with the most likely preference reported as \textsc{Undetermined}. With that judgment, its planning
tool call requests
\texttt{self\_commitments=[Maple], partner\_commitments=[Cedar]}.
Accepting would complete Garden, giving Blair $-1$, so Blair rejects;
Alex's terminal utility is $0$.
With the oracle judgment, the tool call changes to
\texttt{self\_commitments=[], partner\_commitments=[Maple]}.
Blair accepts because Harbor gives it $1$; Alex also receives $1$.
The optimal call would add Maple for both players, completing Orchard,
Harbor, and Library. Its utilities are $1+1+0=2$ for Alex and
$1+1-1=1$ for Blair, so Blair accepts. The three oracle action values are
therefore $0$, $1$, and $2$, respectively. The two recorded planning calls
have regrets $2$ and $1$, giving intervention gain $\Delta_c=1$.
These actions correspond to indices 4, 2, and 5 in the archived legal-action
list. Recomputing the reference policy reproduces the archived policy hash,
posterior, and action values.
