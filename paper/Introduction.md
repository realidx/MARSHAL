## 1 Introduction

Multi-agent Language Model systems are increasingly used for tasks that require agents to exchange information, negotiate responsibilities, and act on one another over multiple turns. Yet stronger individual models do not reliably yield stronger multi-agent systems. Recent studies report failures even when the underlying task is well within the capability of the participating models: agents overlook decisive private information held by others, communicate extensively without converting messages into effective coordination, and lose track of jointly grounded plans across interaction turns (Li et al., 2026a; Zhang et al., 2026b; Yao et al., 2026). Broader analyses similarly identify interaction-level failures as a distinct source of error beyond ordinary single-agent reasoning mistakes (Cemri et al., 2025; Anthropic, 2026). These results point to a gap between being capable in isolation and being capable **in interaction**.

An agent must infer behaviorally relevant latent states of other agents—such as their private preferences, knowledge, or likely behavior—and choose its own interaction accordingly. These two computations are sequentially interdependent. A poor inference induces a poor plan, but the chosen interaction also controls what the partner reveals next and therefore what evidence becomes available for future belief updates. Existing benchmarks expose failures at different points in this loop, but improving one component in isolation does not guarantee effective interaction.

This raises a training question: **what is the right unit for learning this interaction loop?** Games are a natural substrate because they expose utilities, information structure, legal actions, and strategic consequences explicitly. Recent work has therefore used self-play and multi-agent games to train strategic and transferable reasoning in LLMs (Yuan et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026; Lyu et al., 2026; He et al., 2026b). However, full-game trajectories are often a poor training unit for social reasoning. Most states do not require consequential reasoning about another agent, while the few that do are surrounded by long stretches of state tracking, routine actions, and other incidental difficulties（这里想这么说必须得给citation）. In richer social games, like avalon... the opposite problem appears: multiple phenomena—communication, planning, memory, grounding, deception, commitment, and execution—are entangled in the same trajectory, making it difficult to identify which interaction capability generated success or failure.

We propose **strategic subgame** as a more direct training abstraction. A strategic subgame is a compact slice of a larger interaction initialized from a strategically meaningful state. It retains the information available to each player, the relevant latent preferences, the interaction history needed to interpret them, and the future consequences of alternative actions. For a given subgame, we can compute an evidence-supported distribution over latent agent states and evaluate the continuation value of alternative interactions under those states. The same example can therefore supervise both what should be inferred from the interaction history and what should be done given that inference.

To instantiate this idea, we extend commitment-based multi-party negotiation (Benac et al., 2026) with private player preferences and partial information. Players hold private preferences over public goals and interact through binding offers, responses, and commitments. Actions therefore both change the strategic state and reveal evidence about latent partner preferences. At the same time, the environment remains compact enough to support exact belief filtering, oracle partner policies, and counterfactual action evaluation. This allows us to construct stratgic subgames in which the dependence between partner inference and interaction choice is explicit and verifiable rather than implicit in a long trajectory.

We then use the same strategic subgames as training instances. Because their latent states and continuation values are known, they provide detailed supervision for both state inference and interaction decisions. Our central hypothesis is that strategic subgames can train an interaction capability that is more general than the game states on which it is learned. We therefore evaluate not only in-domain performance, but also generalization to unseen strategic configurations and transfer to external multi-agent settings. Asking whether training on controlled game interactions can improve coordination under new information structures and incentive patterns.
