Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as interacting agents that exchange 
information, negotiate decisions, and coordinate their actions over multiple turns. 
Such multi-agent systems (MAS) promise to combine information and capabilities distributed
across multiple agents, yet recent work has exposed substantial failures that arise 
specifically through interaction. Agents fail to proactively acquire information that 
is available only to others \citep{li2026systematic}, communicate actively without 
converting those exchanges into effective distributed reasoning \citep{zhang2026silobench}, 
and remain unable to coordinate effectively even when relevant information is made 
transparent \citep{yao2026talk}. Broader analyses similarly identify inter-agent failures 
as a distinct source of error beyond mistakes made by individual agents in isolation 
\citep{cemri2025multiagent,anthropic2026multiagent}. These findings raise a basic question: 
what capabilities are missing when individually capable agents fail to interact effectively?

Classical models of interactive decision making provide a principled way to make this question 
more precise. In an interactive POMDP, an agent maintains uncertainty not only over the 
physical environment, but also over models of other agents, and plans its own actions 
with respect to this interactive state \citep{gmytrasiewicz2005ipomdp}. Related work 
on opponent modeling similarly couples inference about another agent with planning a 
response to the inferred model \citep{huang2024hop}. Viewed through this lens, 
effective interaction requires at least two computations: \textbf{interactive-state inference}, 
which construct belief of other agents relevant information, and \textbf{interaction planning}, 
which uses that belief to evaluate what to do next. Importantly, these computations are 
coupled over time. An inferred state changes the value of a interaction action; 
the selected interaction then changes how other agents respond and therefore what 
evidence becomes available for subsequent inference. Established models of multi-agent 
decision making identify these two computations as fundamental components of interaction, 
motivating us to ask whether current LLM agents can reliably perform them together \citep{riemer2025tom}.

We study this question in \textbf{incomplete-information, mixed-incentive coordination}. This setting is important both structurally and practically. Incomplete information makes relevant properties of other decision makers latent, creating a genuine inference problem; mixed incentives make those properties consequential for action selection, because an interaction that is beneficial under one counterpart objective may be ineffective or harmful under another. Their combination therefore preserves both sides of the interactive decision problem: the agent must infer whom it is interacting with and reason about how that inference changes the value of its own actions. These conditions arise naturally when agents act on behalf of distinct principals. Scheduling agents coordinate around privately held constraints and individual disruption costs \citep{zou2026calbench}; strategic negotiation agents pursue asymmetric private objectives while forming temporary alliances \citep{oneill2026cooperate}; and agents representing separate economic principals exhibit state-dependent strategic behavior over repeated commercial interactions \citep{li2026emergent}. We therefore use incomplete-information, mixed-incentive interaction not as an attempt to reproduce every form of MAS, but as a controlled and practically meaningful realization of the interactive-decision structure above.

Having identified the capabilities of interest, the next challenge is \textbf{how to diagnose and train them}. Games are an attractive substrate because their information structure, utilities, legal actions, and outcomes can be controlled and verified. Recent work has consequently used multi-agent games and self-play to train strategic or transferable reasoning in LLMs \citep{yuan2026marshal,liu2026spiral,lyu2026gift,he2026stratreasoner}. However, these environments are generally designed to optimize and evaluate game-level behavior rather than to provide separate supervision for interactive-state inference and the planning that follows from it. Scalable self-play often relies on compact competitive or cooperative games that do not simultaneously require incomplete information and mixed incentives, while richer social games can combine deduction, communication, deception, coordination, memory, and long-horizon execution within the same trajectory \citep{light2023avalonbench}. Both are valuable training paradigms, but neither is designed around the diagnostic granularity required here: for a particular interaction, \textbf{what should the agent infer, and what should it do given that inference?}

We construct a controlled game environment to make both quantities explicit. Starting from commitment-based multi-party negotiation \citep{benac2026benchmark}, we introduce private player preferences and incomplete information. Players assign different values to public goals and interact through consequential offers, responses, and binding commitments. Their actions therefore serve two roles at once: they alter future strategic opportunities and provide evidence about the latent preferences that generated them. The environment is deliberately compact. Its purpose is not to maximize surface realism, but to preserve the structural ingredients of incomplete-information, mixed-incentive interaction while remaining small enough for exact filtering over latent agent states, oracle partner policies, and counterfactual evaluation of alternative interactions. This gives us a controlled realization of the classical interactive-decision problem in which both inference and planning can be inspected directly.

We first use this environment to diagnose current LLM agents. At the same decision point, we can separately test whether a model's inference is supported by the interaction history and whether its planned interaction is appropriate under that inference. This exposes errors that final game reward alone cannot distinguish and allows us to examine how inference and planning interact. We then construct \textbf{strategic game slices} around such decision points. Each slice retains the interaction history, latent state, incentive structure, and relevant continuation of the original game, but provides oracle targets for both what can be inferred and how alternative interactions should be valued. The purpose of these slices is not to claim that shortened-game training is generally superior to full-game self-play; they provide a controlled unit for supplying detailed supervision to the specific computations revealed by our diagnosis.

Finally, we train LLM agents using this supervision and ask whether the resulting improvement extends beyond the states on which it was provided. We evaluate generalization to structurally unseen configurations of the training game and transfer to external multi-agent environments with different interaction mechanics. Our aim is therefore broader than producing a stronger negotiation agent: \textbf{we test whether targeted game supervision can improve interaction reasoning that remains useful when the surface game changes.}

Our contributions are threefold:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A controlled diagnosis of interactive reasoning in LLM-based MAS.} Grounded in classical interactive decision making, we study interactive-state inference and interaction planning in incomplete-information, mixed-incentive coordination, and separately measure failures in the two while retaining their sequential dependence.
    \item \textbf{A compact game construction for verifiable social-reasoning supervision.} We show how private preferences, consequential commitments, and information-revealing actions can instantiate a practically relevant interactive-decision problem while providing exact latent-state targets and counterfactual action values.
    \item \textbf{Targeted game supervision and transfer.} We construct strategic game slices around diagnosed interaction decisions, use their oracle structure to supervise inference and planning, and test whether the resulting capabilities generalize to unseen strategic configurations and external multi-agent environments.
\end{itemize}
