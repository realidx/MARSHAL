Large language models (LLMs) increasingly operate in multi-agent systems
that distribute work and coordinate decisions through interaction
\citep{zhu2025multiagentbench,chen2026collabsim}. Yet interaction can fail at
several distinct points. In tasks with distributed information, agents leave
partners' private evidence unexplored \citep{li2026systematic}; in distributed
computation, they exchange messages without turning them into effective joint
reasoning \citep{zhang2026silobench}; and in negotiation, sharing the relevant
information does not by itself ensure a coordinated plan \citep{yao2026talk}.
A broader analysis likewise identifies inter-agent misalignment as a distinct
failure class \citep{cemri2025multiagent}. These findings raise a basic
question: what capabilities are missing for individually capable agents to continuesly fail in interaction?

Interactive decision theory gives this question a precise form. An interactive
POMDP represents uncertainty over both the physical environment and models of
other agents, and selects actions on the basis of beliefs about both
\citep{gmytrasiewicz2005ipomdp}. Opponent-modeling work similarly links
inferences about others' goals or policies to the planning of a response
\citep{carmel1996opponent,huang2024hop}. We therefore distinguish two capabilities:
\textbf{interactive-state inference}, which estimates other agents'
task-relevant latent states from their behavior, and \textbf{interaction
planning}, which uses those estimates to choose an action. We refer to them
as \emph{inference} and \emph{planning} below. These two capabilities form a feedback loop in interaction:
inference informs the next action, while that action changes how others respond
and what can be inferred thereafter. Evidence from LLM agents suggests that
representing or predicting another agent's state need not translate into an
effective response \citep{riemer2025tom,sobotka2026strategic}. This gap
motivates examining inference and planning both separately and together.

Realistic multi-agent interactions are difficult to diagnose because
information, objectives, and decisions are distributed across agents and time.
Coordination around private calendars, negotiation over asymmetric goals, and
commerce on behalf of separate principals illustrate this complexity
\citep{zou2026calbench,oneill2026cooperate,li2026emergent}. Games offer a
controlled setting: their information structure, utilities, legal actions,
and strategic consequences can be specified and verified. Recent work has
used multi-agent games and self-play to train strategic or transferable
reasoning in LLMs
\citep{yuan2026marshal,liu2026spiral,lyu2026gift,he2026stratreasoner}.
Yet a game-level outcome alone does not reveal whether an agent misread a
partner or failed to act on a correct inference. Compact games can simplify
away private information or mixed incentives, while richer social games may
entangle deduction, communication, deception, commitment, and execution in a
single trajectory \citep{light2023avalonbench}. To retain both capabilities
while making them separately measurable, we extend the commitment-based
multi-party negotiation game \citep{benac2026benchmark} with private
preferences and costly investigation action. Incomplete information makes relevant
partner states latent, creating an inference problem; mixed incentives make
those states consequential for planning.

Using this controlled setting, we make the two capabilities separately
testable and trainable in LLM-agent interaction. We first construct matched decision
problems showing that inference and planning are separated capabilities that will affect the interaction,
and they take effect dependently in an long horizon case (Section~\ref{sec:diagnose}).
We then build \emph{game slices} from deliberately structured small games:
contiguous stretches of interaction containing one or more decisions, for
which the reference policy provides verifiable partner-state labels and
counterfactual action values. These slices provide
targeted training signals for decisions that terminal game reward alone cannot
disentangle. Finally, we compare game-slice training with complete-game
self-play in terms of training-token efficiency, evaluate generalization to
held-out game structures, and test transfer to more complex multi-agent coordination environment
(Section~\ref{sec:experiment}).
