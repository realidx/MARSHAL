Large language models (LLMs) increasingly operate in multi-agent systems
that distribute work and coordinate decisions through interaction
\citep{zhu2025multiagentbench,chen2026collabsim}. Yet interaction can fail at
several distinct points. In tasks with distributed information, agents leave
partners' private evidence unexplored \citep{li2026systematic}; in distributed
computation, they exchange messages without turning them into effective joint
reasoning \citep{zhang2026silobench}; and in negotiation, sharing the relevant
information does not by itself ensure a coordinated plan \citep{yao2026talk}.
A broader analysis likewise identifies inter-agent misalignment as a distinct
failure class \citep{cemri2025multiagent}. These findings raise a basic
question: what prevents individually capable agents from coordinating
effectively?

Interactive decision theory provides a starting point. In an interactive
partially observable Markov decision process (I-POMDP), an agent maintains
beliefs about both the physical environment and other agents, and uses those
beliefs to choose actions
\citep{gmytrasiewicz2005ipomdp}. Opponent-modeling work similarly links
inferences about others' goals or policies to the planning of a response
\citep{carmel1996opponent,huang2024hop}. Building on this distinction, we focus
on two capabilities: \emph{interactive-state inference}, which estimates
partners' task-relevant hidden states from the available evidence, and
\emph{interaction planning}, which uses those estimates to evaluate and
select actions. We refer to them as \emph{inference} and \emph{planning}.
They form a feedback loop: an inference informs an action, which shapes
partners' responses and the evidence available for subsequent decisions.
Studies of LLM agents find that accurately predicting another agent's state
or behavior need not lead to an effective response
\citep{riemer2025tom,sobotka2026strategic}. This gap motivates evaluating both
the inference itself and the decisions made from it.

Diagnosing these capabilities in realistic interactions is difficult because
information, objectives, and decisions are distributed across agents and time.
Coordination with private calendars, strategic coalition formation, and
commerce on behalf of different principals illustrate this complexity
\citep{zou2026calbench,oneill2026cooperate,li2026emergent}. Games offer a
controlled setting: their information structure, utilities, legal actions,
and strategic consequences can be specified and verified. Recent work has
used multi-agent games and self-play to train strategic or transferable
reasoning in LLMs
\citep{yuan2026marshal,liu2026spiral,lyu2026gift,he2026stratreasoner}.
Yet a game-level outcome alone does not reveal whether an agent misread a
partner or failed to act on a correct belief. In social deduction games, for
example, a single trajectory combines inference, deception, and collective
decision-making \citep{light2023avalonbench}. We seek a setting that retains
strategic uncertainty while allowing beliefs and actions to be evaluated
separately. To this end, we extend the commitment-based multi-party negotiation
game of \citet{benac2026benchmark} with private preferences and investigation
actions that consume a decision opportunity. Hidden preferences create an
inference problem; their alignment or conflict determines the value of
possible plans.

This setting connects diagnosis, targeted training, and transfer
(Figure~\ref{fig:overview}). We first diagnose Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 by
evaluating inference and planning at the same decision and replacing the
model's explicit inference with an oracle judgment
(Section~\ref{sec:diagnose}). Its choices depend on the supplied judgment,
but correcting that judgment does not reliably resolve planning errors.
We then construct \emph{game slices}: contiguous segments of one or more
decisions from deliberately structured small games. An oracle supplies
partner-preference judgments and counterfactual action values under a fixed
reference policy, providing targeted supervision for action selection,
inference, and conditional planning. We compare slice training with
complete-game self-play in terms of training-token efficiency, generalization
to held-out game structures, and transfer to coordination with private
calendars (Section~\ref{sec:experiment}). In our sequential CalBench
evaluation, the jointly trained model retains more meetings than either
the baseline or self-play, providing evidence that strategic behaviors
learned in small games can transfer to more complex interactions.
