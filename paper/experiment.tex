\label{sec:experiment}

\subsection{Data and training}
\label{sec:experiment-training}

\paragraph{Data generation.}
We construct 100 game slices from the games in Section~\ref{sec:benac-i},
selecting for identifiable inference and planning demands. Inference
coverage includes evidence that changes or preserves a preference judgment
and investigation outcomes that inform subsequent choices. Planning coverage
includes commitments that shape later opportunities and agreements that
depend on multiple partners' responses and interdependent commitments.
We include information contrasts requiring different actions, alongside
controls in which actions should remain unchanged. For scored action tasks,
we require $\varnothing\neq\mathcal A_\epsilon(I)\subsetneq\mathcal L(I)$,
where $\mathcal A_\epsilon(I)$ is the oracle's acceptable-action set under
the scoring tolerance: at least one legal action must be acceptable and one
unacceptable. Slices are executed with the oracle to advance game states
and generate decision-level supervision. Histories are checked by legal replay. The oracle supplies
three supervision types: direct action selection, inference of possible and
most likely preferences, and planning given an oracle judgment. Failed
reference computations are excluded, and planning labels ambiguous under
the supplied judgment are masked. Appendix~\ref{app:slice-selection}
details these structural selection and coverage criteria.

\paragraph{Training.}
All trained variants start from Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507~\citep{yang2025qwen3}. Self-play (SP) learns from each
player's terminal utility in complete games, starting from 100 initial
game configurations. Slice training instead uses
oracle-scored responses: \textsc{GameSlice-A} trains direct action selection,
\textsc{GameSlice-AP} trains direct action selection and conditional planning, and
\textsc{GameSlice-Joint} combines all three tasks. These objectives update
a single model without imposing an inference-to-planning pipeline at test
time. Slice training uses grouped policy optimization with eight responses
per item and a KL penalty toward the frozen base model. We use BF16 and an
initial learning rate of $10^{-6}$. All training variants use a budget of
approximately 6M generated response tokens, and we evaluate the final
checkpoint of each training run. The training settings
are listed in Tables~\ref{tab:training-configuration}
and~\ref{tab:selfplay-configuration} in
Appendix~\ref{app:training}.

\subsection{Same-Model Negotiation}
\label{sec:experiment-team}

We evaluate complete games with two, three, and four players, using 16 fixed
instances per team size and one greedy rollout per instance. All players
use the same checkpoint, each observing only its own private information
and the public history. Instances are shared across models within each size.
We report mean team utility, the sum of players' signed terminal utilities
averaged over games.
Table~\ref{tab:experiment-team} reports the results;
Appendix~\ref{app:team-benac} provides more detailed statistics.

\paragraph{Results.}
All slice-trained variants exceed the baseline's mean utility in each suite,
but adding supervision objectives does not yield monotonic gains. Self-play
remains close to the baseline. In four-player games, \textsc{GameSlice-A} secures more
accepted offers than Self-play despite making fewer proposals (38/59 versus
25/78). One rollout shows how successive bilateral agreements accumulate
the commitments needed for two goals, yielding team utility 4 versus Baseline's 0.


\subsection{Transfer to Sequential Calendar Coordination}
\label{sec:experiment-transfer}

CalBench~\citep{zou2026calbench} evaluates decentralized meeting coordination:
agents with private calendars and rescheduling costs communicate to agree on
meeting times. A feasible agreement requires agents to learn their partners'
constraints and reconcile them with their own schedules, potentially moving
private errands or existing meetings. We evaluate transfer on a 24-game
sequential adaptation, with four agents coordinating three meetings per
game. Meetings arrive sequentially, so accommodating a new request must also
account for earlier commitments. Each team uses the same model for all
agents, and all models face identical scenarios and evaluation settings.
Detailed environment and inference settings are provided in
Appendix~\ref{app:calbench-setting}.

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
We report retained meetings (out of 72), fully successful games (out of 24),
per-agent excess rescheduling cost, communication overhead, and burden
imbalance. Success is determined from the final calendars. Excess cost is
measured relative to the minimum feasible team cost and averaged only over
successful games. A retained meeting must occupy a common slot for every
participant; a successful game must retain all three meetings while
preserving blocked calendar items. Communication counts sent direct messages
per agent, divided by its round-level scheduling successes (with denominator
at least one). Imbalance measures deviations in per-agent excess burdens
relative to a reference for the round-successful meeting subset. Both include
failed games. We additionally report Qwen3-4B (thinking), MARSHAL
Generalist~\citep{yuan2026marshal}, and Social-R1-4B~\citep{wu2026socialr1}
as a separate comparison group. Exact metric definitions and model details
appear in Appendix~\ref{app:calbench-setting}.

\paragraph{Results.}
Table~\ref{tab:experiment-calbench} shows that \textsc{GameSlice-Joint}
retains 47 meetings, compared with 37 for both the baseline and self-play,
and completes nine games compared with five and six, respectively. It also
reduces communication and burden imbalance relative to both, suggesting
that its higher completion rate is accompanied by more efficient
coordination and a more even distribution of costs.
\textsc{GameSlice-AP} achieves the most complete games and the lowest
conditional excess cost, although cost averages cover different successful
subsets for each model. Both variants retain more meetings than action-only
training, supporting the value of adding conditional planning supervision
for transfer to this coordination task. A matched rollout makes the behavioral difference concrete. After learning
that a proposed slot is blocked, \textsc{GameSlice-Joint} checks a partner's
alternative with the remaining participant. Later, the agents identify a
conflict with an existing meeting and agree on another slot, retaining all
three meetings. Under the same initial calendars, the baseline and self-play
teams fail to establish a common slot for the second meeting and retain only
the first. This trajectory illustrates two demands of coordination: using a
partner's response to revise the feasible options, and checking a proposed
revision against other participants' constraints and existing commitments.
Appendix~\ref{app:calbench-rollout} presents the detailed comparison
with verbatim model outputs.
