\paragraph{Self-play training.}
All players act using the current policy, with each receiving only its own
observation and available interaction history. Training collects complete
games and assigns each player's terminal utility to that player's decisions.
The environment supplies the reward; oracle judgments and action labels are
not used. Four stochastic trajectories are collected for each sampled initial
game, with returns associated with the same initial game and player seat.
The number of decisions varies across trajectories, so collection is governed
by generated response tokens rather than a fixed number of decision items.

\subsection{Structural criteria for slice selection}
\label{app:slice-selection}

Strategic coverage concerns the relationship between available evidence,
partner responses, and the consequences of a decision. We distinguish
eligibility checks, verified relations between candidate decisions, and
structural coverage dimensions (Table~\ref{tab:slice-selection}). These
criteria describe candidate construction and coverage; they are not an
exhaustive specification of subsequent training-pool selection. Coverage is
defined across the collection, rather than requiring every slice to exhibit
every property.

For a decision information set $I$, let $\mathcal A_\epsilon(I)$ denote the
legal actions accepted by the oracle scorer, retaining all actions within
the utility tolerance $\epsilon=0.1$ and applying the stated response
tie-breaking rule. Action-supervision eligibility requires
$\varnothing\neq\mathcal A_\epsilon(I)\subsetneq\mathcal L(I)$.
This excludes decisions with no reliable acceptable action or with no
distinction between acceptable and unacceptable choices. It does not require
a unique best action, and it is not a filter on inference-only questions.

For an information contrast $(I,I')$, disjoint acceptable-action sets,
$\mathcal A_\epsilon(I)\cap\mathcal A_\epsilon(I')=\varnothing$, certify
that no single action is acceptable in both conditions. Such contrasts need
not be consecutive decisions: a contiguous slice follows one legal
trajectory, whereas counterfactual branches provide related supervision
items. Cross-turn and multi-party coverage are structural descriptions;
neither a longer remaining horizon nor a larger player count alone certifies
that short-horizon or single-partner reasoning would fail. We do not impose
such an ablation-based requirement on every selected slice.

\subsection{Illustrative training prompts}
\label{app:training-prompts}

The templates below summarize the instructions and information supplied to
the model. They are explanatory renderings of the training prompts, with
instance-specific content replaced by bracketed placeholders. We use the
paper's terminology for preference judgments rather than implementation
field names. Slices are executed with the oracle, which advances the game
and updates each player's information along the trajectory. The resulting
decision points supply the model's supervision requests. The three templates
describe supervision views of those decisions; a model-generated inference
response is not automatically passed to the planning view, which receives
the oracle judgment.

\paragraph{Shared context and response format.}
Each slice prompt specifies the acting player, game rules, goal requirements,
own preferences, binding commitments, pending offer (if any), remaining
proposal order, and investigation budget. Goal requirements describe the
commitments needed for completion, not players' preferences. The prompt
also states the reference partner policy, including its response tie-breaking
rule. Legal actions and their arguments are supplied through tool schemas.
The shared system instruction is:
\begin{quote}\small
You are answering a reasoning question about a negotiation game. Use the
stated rules and visible information. Briefly explain your answer, then
make exactly one requested tool call.
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Direct action selection.}
The model receives the public history and its own private observations,
together with the public preference prior. No oracle judgment is supplied.
\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{Context:} [Game rules, goals, own preferences, public prior, partner
policy, visible interaction history, private investigation answers, and
current decision state.]\\[2pt]
\textbf{Task:} Choose your next action. Pursue the goals you want and avoid
bringing about goals you dislike. When accepting and rejecting are equally
good for you, choose the response that helps the other players more.\\[2pt]
\textbf{Response:} Briefly explain your decision, then make exactly one legal
game-action tool call. Keep the explanation outside the tool arguments.
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Inference.}
This request uses the available evidence to query a particular partner's
preference for a particular goal. Its output is a judgment, not a game action.
\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{Context:} [Game and decision information, public prior, partner
policy, visible history, and private observations.]\\[2pt]
\textbf{Task:} Assess [partner]'s preference for [goal]. Use the observed
behavior and revealed information under the stated partner policy. Report
every preference still possible, including unlikely ones, and the most likely
preference, or \texttt{undetermined} when no preference is clearly better
supported under the specified reporting rule.\\[2pt]
\textbf{Response:} Briefly explain, then make exactly one
\texttt{SUBMIT\_BELIEFS} call containing the requested judgment.
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Planning given an oracle judgment.}
Here, the oracle judgment is an input; the model must choose an action
conditioned on that judgment. The template shows the planning view that
supplies the current decision state and qualitative judgments directly,
without requiring reconstruction of the evidence history.
\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{Context:} [Game rules, goals, own preferences, partner policy, current
decision state, and legal actions.]\\[2pt]
\textbf{Supplied oracle judgment:} [For each specified partner--goal pair:
possible preferences and most likely preference, or \texttt{undetermined}.]\\[2pt]
\textbf{Task:} Use the supplied judgment directly to choose your next action.
Pursue your own goals under the stated continuation policy, including its
response tie-breaking rule.\\[2pt]
\textbf{Response:} Briefly explain your decision, then make exactly one legal
game-action tool call.
\end{quote}
Both action tasks also state the scoring tolerance: an action may lose at
most $0.1$ utility relative to the best action; for responses tied in own
utility, the corresponding allowance in other players' total utility is
$0.1$. The acceptable-action labels remain with the scorer and are not
included in the model's input.

\paragraph{Self-play.}
At each decision in a complete game, the acting player receives the game
rules, its own preferences and private observations, the public history,
and the current state and legal tools. Its instruction is summarized below.
\begin{quote}\small
Your objective is to maximize your own expected final score. Other players'
preferences may differ from yours. Briefly explain your decision using the
information available to you, then make exactly one legal game-action tool
call. Your explanation is private and is not shown to other players.
\end{quote}
The selected action advances the game, and the next player receives its own
observation. Unlike the slice tasks, these requests contain no supplied
oracle judgment or reference-policy action target; training feedback comes
from the player's terminal utility.
